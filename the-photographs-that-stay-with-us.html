PERENNIAL PORTRAITS · BEHIND THE PHOTOGRAPH

The Photographs
That Stay With Us.

A return to Sister Julianne's portrait, and a reminder of why the simplest photographs can become the ones we treasure most.

Sometimes, you have to go back and look again.

I photographed Sister Julianne some time ago and had previously shared a few photographs from this session. Last night, while going through her RAW images again, I came across this particular frame. It stopped me. I found myself looking at it for a little longer than I expected.

There was something about this photograph that I hadn't fully appreciated before. It was captured in black and white, and what stood out most was the way Sister Julianne looked directly into the camera. Her smile, her expression and the quiet confidence in her presence came together in a way that felt completely natural.

There was no need for an elaborate setup or anything particularly dramatic. Just a person in front of the camera, allowing a little of who they are to come through.

The beauty of keeping things simple

In today's photography world, we are surrounded by dramatic edits, beautiful locations, elaborate lighting setups and the latest trends. There is always something new to try, another style gaining attention, and another reason to make an image stand out.

Perhaps a photograph like this might seem simple, even boring, to some. It doesn't rely on a complicated concept or a fashionable treatment. It asks very little of the viewer, except to stop for a moment and look.

But these are precisely the photographs that mean so much to me.

I love portraits that don't necessarily demand attention but invite you to spend a moment with them. Images where the expression, the eyes and the personality of the person become the most important part of the frame. The lighting and composition matter, of course, but they should support the person in the photograph, never compete with them.

More than a photograph for today

I often think about what happens to our photographs years from now. The people who will hold them, the stories they will tell, and the memories they will bring back. Children grow up, families change, and eventually photographs become some of the most meaningful connections we have to the people and moments that shaped our lives.

I hope this photograph finds a permanent place in Sister Julianne's family albums and remains there for generations. Perhaps one day, a grandchild or great-grandchild will come across it and spend a moment looking at the woman in the photograph. They may not know anything about the day it was taken, but they will have something tangible to hold on to.

They will be able to see her smile, study her expression and perhaps recognise something familiar in her face. That is a beautiful thing for a photograph to offer.

Why I keep coming back to portraits like this

Trends will change. Styles will evolve. Cameras, lenses and editing tools will come and go. What looks current today may feel dated in a few years. But the value of a photograph that preserves someone you love does not depend on any of those things.

A portrait can become part of a family's story. It can sit on a wall, live in an album, be passed from one generation to the next, and bring someone close again through a familiar face. We cannot know exactly what an image will mean to the people who see it in the future. We can only take care in making it today.

That thought means more to me than any number of likes or comments ever could. It is the reason I keep returning to photographs like this one, and the reason I continue to love the quiet, honest side of portrait photography.

A thought worth keeping

Not every photograph needs to shout to be remembered. Sometimes, it simply needs to preserve a person as they were, in a moment that was entirely their own.

At Perennial Portraits, part of Randall Adams Photography, I believe in creating portraits with care and intention. Photographs that feel honest today, and may mean even more to the people who keep them tomorrow.

Because the greatest reward is knowing that an image might remain in a family long after the day it was made.
