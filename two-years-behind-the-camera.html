A PERSONAL REFLECTION

Two Years
Behind the Camera.

Two years of learning to see, learning to slow down, and discovering that the photographs that matter most are rarely just about how someone looks.

Two years can pass quickly. But when I look back at the beginning of this photography journey, I can see just how much can change in that time.

When I started, I knew I loved photography. I was drawn to the way a photograph could hold a moment still, preserve a face, or bring back a feeling long after the day itself had passed. What I did not fully understand yet was everything that sits behind a finished image: the decisions, the patience, the uncertainty, and the hours spent learning how to make what I imagined match what I could create.

Photography has a way of keeping you humble. There is always another technique to learn, another light to understand, another way to approach a portrait. Some days the progress is obvious. On other days, you look at your work and realise you still have a long way to go. I have had both kinds of days, and I suspect they are both necessary.

Finding my way

Over time, I have become more thoughtful about the photographs I want to make. I am learning to pay attention not only to the light and composition, but to the person in front of the camera: their expression, their character, the way they settle into a moment when they no longer feel they have to perform for the photograph.

My work has taken me into portraits, family sessions, couples, matric celebrations and weddings. Each brings a different pace and a different kind of responsibility. A studio portrait asks for patience and attention to small details. A family session asks you to notice the connections happening between people. A wedding moves quickly, and you have to be ready when an important moment happens once and is gone.

Those experiences have taught me that making a photograph is not simply about pressing the shutter at the right time. It is about being present enough to recognise what is worth keeping.

The photographs are about people

One of the most meaningful parts of this journey has been seeing how much thought can go into a single portrait. Sometimes the idea begins well before the session itself: the mood, the setting, the clothes, or even finding a simple object that helps tell the story. I remember preparing an old book as a prop for a portrait session, asking family for help finding one with the right character. It was a small detail, but it reminded me how much care can live behind a photograph that may look effortless when it is finished.

And then there are the people who trust me to photograph them. That trust matters. Not everyone steps in front of a camera feeling confident. My job is not only to make a good image, but to create space for someone to feel comfortable enough to be themselves. When that happens, the photograph can become something more honest than a pose.

That is what continues to draw me to portraiture. Years from now, the details of a particular day may fade, but a photograph can bring a person back to you in a way that words sometimes cannot.

Still learning, still becoming

There have been challenges, moments of doubt, and times when it is easy to compare your work to someone else's. It takes discipline to remember that every photographer is on a different path, with different experiences and a different eye. I am learning to measure progress against where I began, rather than against someone else's finished chapter.

Building Randall Adams Photography and developing Perennial Portraits have made this journey feel more real. They are not simply names or pages on a website. They represent the kind of work I want to keep making: considered portraits, genuine connection, and photographs that feel just as meaningful long after they are taken.

I still have a great deal to learn. I want to keep refining my approach, become more consistent, and grow in the way I work with people. I want every session to teach me something and every finished photograph to reflect the care that went into making it.

What comes next

When I think about the next two years, I do not have every step mapped out. What I do have is a clearer sense of why I picked up the camera in the first place. I want to make photographs that last. Not images that simply look good for a moment, but photographs that gather meaning as the years pass.

To every person, couple and family who has stood in front of my camera and trusted me with a small piece of their story: thank you. You have been part of this journey, and every session has helped shape the photographer I am becoming.

Two years behind the camera has taught me that the best work comes from staying curious, caring about the people in front of the lens, and being willing to begin again with every photograph.

The most beautiful photographs of this journey may not even have been taken yet.
